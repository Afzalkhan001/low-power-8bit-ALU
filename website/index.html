Design and simulation of a low-power 8-bit ALU

An arithmetic logic unit is the part of a processor that does the math. This one adds, subtracts, multiplies, compares, shifts and runs logic operations on two 8-bit numbers. It is built from 3,738 transistors, and idle blocks are switched off at their inputs to save power.

Abstract

This project designs an 8-bit arithmetic logic unit (ALU) at transistor level in CMOS and simulates it with Cadence Spectre using the 45 nm Generic PDK (gpdk045). The ALU takes two 8-bit operands, A and B, and a 4-bit opcode. It performs 16 operations: addition, subtraction, increment, decrement, 8×8 multiplication with a 16-bit product, comparison, seven bitwise logic operations, two shifts and a rotate. It produces carry, zero, negative and overflow flags.

The datapath uses 28-transistor mirror full adders in a ripple-carry chain, an array multiplier, and transmission-gate multiplexers. To lower power, the design uses operand isolation: the opcode decoder enables one functional block at a time, and AND gates hold the inputs of the idle blocks at zero so they do not switch. Other techniques are minimum-size devices, gated control signals and supply-voltage scaling. Each block has its own supply rail so its power can be measured separately.

The circuit was checked exhaustively at gate level (16 operations × 65,536 operand pairs) and at switch level on the full transistor netlist. A browser-based virtual lab shows every internal bit, how signals propagate, and the estimated energy saved by isolation.

Objectives

  1. Design a 16-operation, 8-bit ALU from CMOS transistors.
  2. Lower dynamic power with operand isolation, small devices and voltage scaling.
  3. Simulate it in Cadence Spectre and check every operation against a golden model.
  4. Measure delay, average power per block and energy per operation.
  5. Compare the design with isolation on and off, and at 1.0 V and 0.8 V.

What are we building?

Your phone and laptop have a processor (CPU). Inside every processor there is a small part that does the actual math. It is called the ALU, short for Arithmetic Logic Unit.

  • Arithmetic means math: add, subtract, multiply.
  • Logic means yes/no operations on bits: AND, OR, NOT and others.
  • Unit means it is one block of the chip.

Our ALU is 8-bit: it works on numbers that are 8 binary digits long (0 to 255). It does 16 operations. You choose the operation with a 4-bit code called the opcode.

VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) is the field of building circuits with thousands to billions of transistors on one chip. This project is a small VLSI design: 3,738 transistors.

Low-power means we design it to use as little energy as possible, so batteries last longer and the chip stays cool.

Think of it like this: the ALU is a calculator with 16 buttons. A and B are the two numbers you type. The opcode is the button you press. Y is the answer on the screen. The flags are little warning lights, such as "the answer was zero" or "the answer was too big".

Bits and binary numbers

A chip is made of switches. A switch is either off or on, so a chip stores information as 0 (off, 0 volts) and 1 (on, the supply voltage, 1 volt in our chip). One 0-or-1 is a bit. Eight bits make a byte.

In everyday numbers each place is worth 10× the one to its right (ones, tens, hundreds). In binary each place is worth 2× the one to its right:

128 · 64 · 32 · 16 · 8 · 4 · 2 · 1

To read a binary number, add up the place values where there is a 1. For example, 00101101 = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45.

  • The leftmost bit (worth 128) is the MSB, most significant bit. We call it bit 7.
  • The rightmost bit (worth 1) is the LSB, least significant bit, bit 0.
  • 8 bits can hold 0 to 255, which is 256 different values.
  • Hexadecimal (hex) is a short way to write binary: every 4 bits become one symbol 0–9 or A–F. So 0010 1101 = 2D.

Adding in binary

You add binary numbers the same way you learned in school: column by column, right to left, carrying when a column gets too big. There are only four cases:

ColumnSumWriteCarry to next column
0 + 0000
0 + 1110
1 + 1201
1 + 1 + 1 (with carry)311

In the ALU, each column is done by a small circuit called a full adder. Eight of them sit in a row, and each one passes its carry to the next. This is called ripple carry, because the carry ripples from right to left like a wave.

If the answer needs a 9th bit (more than 255), that extra bit comes out as the carry flag C = 1. For example, 255 + 1 gives 00000000 with C = 1.

Negative numbers and subtraction

A chip has no minus sign, so it needs a trick to store negative numbers. The trick used everywhere is called two's complement. To get −x from x:

  1. Flip every bit (0 becomes 1, 1 becomes 0). This is NOT x.
  2. Add 1.

With 8 bits, two's complement covers −128 to +127. Any number whose bit 7 is 1 is negative, which is why bit 7 is also the negative flag N.

This makes subtraction easy: A − B = A + (NOT B) + 1. The ALU does not need a separate subtractor. It flips B with XOR gates, sets the adder's first carry-in to 1, and uses the same adder.

For subtraction the carry flag means "no borrow": C = 1 when A ≥ B. The overflow flag V turns on when the signed answer does not fit in −128…127, for example 127 + 1.

Think of it like this: a car's odometer at 000 that rolls back one step shows 999. In 8 bits, 0 − 1 rolls back to 11111111, and the chip treats that as −1.

Multiplying in binary

Binary multiplication is long multiplication like in school, but simpler, because every digit of B is 0 or 1:

  • If a bit of B is 1, write A (shifted to that bit's place).
  • If it is 0, write zeros.
  • Add all the rows.

Each row is called a partial product. Making one partial-product bit is just an AND gate: aj AND bi. Our 8×8 multiplier uses 64 AND gates for the partial products and 56 full adders to add the rows. This arrangement is called an array multiplier.

8 bits × 8 bits can give a 16-bit answer (255 × 255 = 65,025), so the ALU has a second output, Y_HI, for the upper 8 bits.

Logic gates and bitwise operations

A logic gate takes one or two bits in and gives one bit out by a fixed rule. A truth table lists the output for every possible input.

  • AND: 1 only if both inputs are 1.
  • OR: 1 if at least one input is 1.
  • NOT: flips the bit.
  • XOR (exclusive OR): 1 if the inputs are different.
  • NAND, NOR, XNOR: the same as AND, OR and XOR followed by NOT.

A bitwise operation applies a gate to each of the 8 bit positions separately. So A AND B is eight AND gates working side by side.

Shifts move all bits one place. Shift left (SHL) doubles a number, shift right (SHR) halves it. Rotate left (ROL) is a shift left where the bit falling off the left end comes back in on the right.

NAND and NOR are called universal gates because any other gate can be built from them. In CMOS they are also the cheapest gates, which is why the ALU's logic unit is built around NAND, NOR and XNOR.

Transistors: the switches inside

A MOSFET transistor is a switch controlled by a voltage. It has three main terminals: the gate (the control), the source and the drain (the two ends of the switch). There are two kinds:

  • NMOS: the switch closes (conducts) when the gate is 1. NMOS is good at pulling a wire down to 0 (ground).
  • PMOS: the switch closes when the gate is 0. It is drawn with a small circle on the gate. PMOS is good at pulling a wire up to 1 (VDD).

CMOS (Complementary MOS) uses both kinds in pairs. In every gate a PMOS network connects the output to VDD and an NMOS network connects it to ground, and only one of them is on at a time. Because there is never a direct path from VDD to ground when the inputs are steady, CMOS uses almost no power while it is not switching. That is why nearly every chip is CMOS.

The simplest CMOS gate is the inverter (NOT gate): one PMOS on top, one NMOS below. Try it on the right.

A transmission gate is an NMOS and a PMOS side by side, used as a two-way switch that passes a signal through. The ALU uses them to build multiplexers with fewer transistors.

The numbers in our netlist, like w=120n l=45n, are the transistor's width and length in nanometres. 45 nm is the technology size. Smaller widths mean less capacitance and less power.

Building blocks: adder, mux, decoder

Full adder

Adds three bits (a, b and the carry coming in) and gives a sum bit S and a carry out. Its rules: S = a XOR b XOR cin, and Cout = 1 when at least two of the three inputs are 1. Our full adder is the 28-transistor mirror adder, a standard low-power CMOS design.

Multiplexer (mux)

A selector switch. It has several data inputs and a select input that chooses which one reaches the output. A 4:1 mux uses 2 select bits to pick 1 of 4 inputs. The ALU's output mux picks which block's answer goes to Y.

Decoder

Turns a code into "one wire on". Our opcode decoder looks at OP[3:0] and turns on exactly one of four enable wires: arithmetic, multiplier, logic or shifter.

Buffer

Two inverters in a row. The output equals the input, but with a fresh, strong signal. It is used after transmission gates, whose output gets weaker as it passes through.

Putting the ALU together

Now every piece has a name. Here is what happens when you give the ALU two numbers and an opcode:

  1. The decoder reads the opcode and switches on one block.
  2. The isolation gates (AND gates) let A and B into that block only. The other blocks see all zeros.
  3. The block does its job: the arithmetic unit (adder), the multiplier, the logic unit or the shifter.
  4. The output mux sends that block's answer to Y.
  5. The flags are made from the answer.
FlagNameIt is 1 when…Example
CCarrythe answer did not fit in 8 bits (ADD), or there was no borrow (SUB), or a bit fell off (shift)255 + 1
ZZeroevery bit of the answer is 05 − 5
NNegativebit 7 of the answer is 13 − 5
VOverflowthe signed answer is outside −128…127127 + 1
WEWrite enablethe answer should be saved (0 only for CMP)CMP

CMP (compare) is a subtraction where only the flags matter. Z = 1 means A equals B, and C = 1 means A ≥ B. Processors use this for "if" statements.

Power: why "low-power" matters

Every wire in a chip acts like a tiny capacitor, a small bucket of charge. When a wire switches from 0 to 1, charge flows in from the supply. When it switches back to 0, that charge is dumped to ground as heat. So a chip uses energy every time a bit flips. This is called dynamic power:

P = α · C · V² · f

  • α (alpha, activity): how often the wires flip. Fewer flips, less power.
  • C: capacitance, the size of the buckets. Smaller transistors and shorter wires, less power.
  • V: supply voltage. It is squared, so lowering V helps the most. 0.8 V instead of 1.0 V saves 36%.
  • f: clock frequency, how many operations per second.

Operand isolation lowers α. Without it, A and B reach all four blocks, so the multiplier works hard even when you only asked for an AND. With it, idle blocks get zeros and their wires stay still.

Think of it like this: a house where every room's lights turn on whenever anyone uses any room. Operand isolation is a switch per room: only the room in use gets light.

A glitch is a wire that flips more than once before it settles, because inputs arrive at different times. Glitches waste energy too. The virtual lab counts them.

Cadence and simulation

Making a real chip costs a lot and takes months, so engineers first simulate it on a computer. Cadence is a company whose software most chip designers use. The pieces used in this project:

  • PDK (Process Design Kit): files from the chip factory describing how its transistors behave. We use gpdk045, a free teaching kit for a 45 nm process.
  • Virtuoso: the program where you draw schematics, which are circuit diagrams made of transistors and wires.
  • Netlist: the circuit as a text file. Every line says which transistor connects to which wires. Our .scs files are netlists.
  • Spectre: the circuit simulator. It solves the electrical equations and computes every voltage over time.
  • Transient analysis: a simulation over time. We apply 30 input patterns, 10 ns each, and watch the outputs.
  • Testbench: the circuit around the design that feeds it inputs (voltage sources) and loads its outputs.
  • ADE Explorer: the window in Virtuoso where you set up and run simulations.
  • Waveform: a graph of a voltage against time. A digital 1 shows as high, 0 as low.
  • OCEAN: Cadence's scripting language. Our script checks all results automatically.

Before Cadence, this project also checks the design with Python: once at gate level for all 1,048,576 input combinations, and once on the transistor netlist with every transistor treated as an on/off switch.
